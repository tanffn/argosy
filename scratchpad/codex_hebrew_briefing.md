### 3. German citizenship

#### U.S.–Germany treaty

Treaty benefits primarily follow **fiscal domicile**, not nationality alone. Article 1 covers estates and gifts of persons domiciled in the United States or Germany. Article 4 treats someone as German-domiciled not only when she has a German home or habitual abode, but also when she is otherwise subject to unlimited German inheritance and gift tax. [Treaty Articles 1 and 4](https://www.germany.info/us-en/service/09-taxes/taxes-estates-954266).

Consequently:

- If Noga has been permanently outside Germany for more than five years, has no German residence or habitual abode, and is not otherwise subject to unlimited German tax, German citizenship alone does not unlock the treaty.
- If she is still within the five-year emigrant tail, she may be deemed German-domiciled for treaty purposes even though she actually lives in Israel.
- If Noga is treaty-domiciled in Germany when she dies, Article 10(5) can supply the pro-rata U.S. unified credit. The calculation requires her worldwide gross estate and cannot be done from the U.S. portfolio alone.
- More importantly, ordinary shares not connected with U.S. real estate, a permanent establishment, or a covered partnership appear to fall under Article 9’s residual-property rule, which assigns taxation only to the treaty-domicile country. For a qualifying German-domiciled, non-U.S. decedent, that may eliminate U.S. estate tax on ordinary U.S. shares rather than merely enlarge the credit. This point warrants a U.S.–German treaty specialist because it could change the entire Noga-first result.

The treaty provision commonly described as marital relief is a **marital deduction**, not simply a marital credit. Article 10(6) requires both the decedent and survivor to be domiciled in Germany or the United States, subject to its election and waiver requirements. If Ariel is solely Israeli-domiciled, Noga’s German status alone does not satisfy both conditions—regardless of who dies first.

#### German inheritance and gift tax

German ErbStG §2 imposes unlimited liability on the entire transfer if the decedent, donor **or recipient** is an *Inländer*. That includes:

- Anyone retaining a German residence or habitual abode.
- A German citizen who has been permanently abroad for no more than five years, despite having no German residence.
- Certain German public-service employees abroad under separate rules.

Therefore, if Noga left Germany within the five-year period, her worldwide estate can be within German inheritance tax. An interspousal gift from Ariel to Noga can also be German-taxable because the recipient herself is an *Inländer*. The ordinary spouse exemption is EUR 500,000, aggregated with relevant prior transfers; a proposed transfer of roughly half this portfolio would exceed it. [ErbStG §2](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/erbstg_1974/__2.html) and [§16 spouse exemption](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/erbstg_1974/__16.html).

After more than five years abroad, with no German residence, citizenship alone generally no longer creates worldwide liability. Nevertheless, for up to ten years, AStG §§2 and 4 can create an **extended limited** inheritance-tax exposure if she:

- Was a German citizen subject to unlimited German income tax for at least five of the preceding ten years;
- Moved to a low-tax jurisdiction or no tax jurisdiction;
- Retains substantial German economic interests; and
- Meets the statutory income or asset thresholds.

That extended regime is broader than ordinary German-situs taxation but is not automatically worldwide taxation. Israel is not automatically “low tax”; the statutory comparison and Noga’s actual tax burden must be tested. The essential facts are her exact German departure date, whether a German home remains available, her German assets/income, and where the intended heirs live.
